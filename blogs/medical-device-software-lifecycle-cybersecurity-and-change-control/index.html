Medical device software lifecycle: cybersecurity, SBOMs and change control

A software engineering overview of medical device software lifecycle controls, dependency visibility, vulnerability response and controlled updates.

By GrapeCrux Editorial Team

Scope note: This article is a general engineering overview, not regulatory, medical or quality-system advice. Device manufacturers must obtain qualified regulatory and quality guidance for their intended market and product.

Software in or connected to a medical device has a lifecycle that extends beyond its first release. Dependencies, operating environments and security threats change after a product is marketed. Engineering teams need an organised process for risk assessment, component visibility, vulnerability handling, testing and updates. Requirements vary by product, intended use and jurisdiction.

Build lifecycle controls into design

Record software requirements, architecture, hazards, security assumptions and verification evidence. Track dependencies and interfaces. Risk analysis should consider how a software failure or cybersecurity event could affect the device and related systems. Product teams should involve quality, regulatory, clinical and security specialists early.

Maintain a software bill of materials

An SBOM helps identify commercial, open-source and off-the-shelf components used in a build. Keep component versions and identifiers linked to each release. A list alone does not fix vulnerable software, but it makes exposure analysis and coordinated remediation more manageable.

Manage vulnerabilities after release

Monitor credible vulnerability sources, assess whether affected components are present and determine the relevance to the product configuration. Define severity, exploitability and potential safety impact with appropriate domain expertise. Maintain a coordinated disclosure and remediation process, with clear customer and stakeholder communication.

Control changes and updates

Evaluate whether a change affects security, intended behaviour, compatibility, safety or evidence already produced. Test updates in representative configurations and document the rationale. Make update distribution and rollback responsibilities clear, especially when devices operate in environments with limited connectivity.

Design secure build and support processes

Protect source repositories, signing keys, build infrastructure and release permissions. Verify software artifacts and maintain traceability between requirements, tests and released versions. Establish a support and end-of-life policy so users understand the maintenance expectations.

Use current official guidance

Regulatory recommendations are updated over time. The US FDA published revised medical-device cybersecurity guidance in February 2026. Organisations must check the current guidance and applicable rules for the markets and device classes they serve rather than relying on a generic checklist.

Key takeaway: For medical device software, component transparency, vulnerability response and controlled updates are continuing lifecycle responsibilities.
