The semantic layer: trusted metrics for analytics and AI assistants

An AI assistant can generate a syntactically valid query and still answer the wrong business question. A semantic layer helps teams define measures, dimensions and business rules once so dashboards, analysts and AI workflows use the same meaning.

Why shared business definitions matter

Different teams can use the same words for different calculations. Revenue might mean gross bookings, recognized revenue or net revenue after refunds. Active customer might mean a login, paid account or qualifying transaction. If each dashboard or AI prompt implements the definition independently, the organization gets conflicting answers.

A semantic layer creates a governed interface between raw data and business questions. It defines measures, dimensions, relationships, filters and calculation rules in business language while keeping SQL and storage details out of every report.

Model metrics with owners and tests

Give each important metric a definition, owner, calculation, grain, approved dimensions, freshness target and known exclusions. Specify how time zones, refunds, currency conversion, late-arriving events and slowly changing dimensions are handled.

Add checks for uniqueness, nulls, relationships, accepted values and reconciliation against source systems. Test at the grain where the metric is consumed. A revenue measure correct by month may still be wrong by individual order line.

Use semantic definitions as an AI boundary

Natural-language analytics introduces another consumer of business data. Instead of letting a model invent SQL over raw warehouse tables, provide approved metrics, dimensions and query operations. Validate queries against an allowlist and user authorization before execution.

The model can translate a question into a structured request, but the semantic service should enforce valid combinations, row-level access and query limits. Return metric definitions and filters with results so an assistant can explain what it calculated.

Roll out a small governed model

Provide a searchable catalog, examples of approved metrics and a process to propose new definitions. Version changes and identify dependent dashboards and AI applications. Keep experimental measures separate from production metrics until business owners review them.

  • Choose high-value metrics with conflicting definitions.
  • Agree on grain, meaning, owner and exclusions.
  • Encode measures and dimensions in a shared model.
  • Add quality, reconciliation and permission tests.
  • Connect one dashboard and one AI analytics workflow.
  • Expand only after business owners adopt the definitions.

Example: define revenue once across reports and AI

Imagine finance defines net revenue as recognized sales minus refunds and credits, excluding taxes and internal test transactions. Sales dashboards, monthly reports and a natural-language analytics assistant should all call the same governed measure. The definition should specify currency conversion, accounting date, late adjustments, grain and exclusions. A user asking “What was revenue last month?” should receive the metric name, period, filters and relevant freshness status with the answer.

The semantic service must also enforce authorization. A model should not be able to bypass row-level or column-level rules by composing a different SQL query over raw tables. Return only approved result shapes and limit query cost and scope.

Governance questions for metric owners

  • Who owns the business meaning and approves definition changes?
  • What is the grain, time zone, currency and treatment of late data?
  • Which dimensions and filters are valid?
  • Which user groups can see the measure and its underlying detail?
  • Which dashboards, extracts and AI workflows depend on it?

Version the definition and publish changes with an effective date so business teams can understand why results may differ over time.
