Healthcare interoperability with HL7 FHIR: a practical software architecture guide

Understand how healthcare systems exchange structured information using FHIR resources, profiles, terminology, identity and consent-aware APIs.

By GrapeCrux Editorial Team

Scope note: This article is about software architecture and interoperability. It is not clinical guidance, does not interpret patient data and does not replace local legal or healthcare governance review.

Healthcare organisations use many systems for appointments, clinical records, laboratory results, medication workflows, billing and patient communication. Interoperability is not simply connecting two APIs. It requires shared data meaning, identity matching, access control, provenance and operational agreements. HL7 FHIR provides a widely used framework for representing and exchanging healthcare information, but local implementation guides and governance still matter.

Start with the workflow and data purpose

Describe the information that needs to move, who creates it, who consumes it and which decision or workflow depends on it. A referral, observation and medication request have different meanings and lifecycle expectations. Define the business process before selecting resources or building integration endpoints.

Understand resources and profiles

FHIR represents information through resources such as Patient, Practitioner, Observation, Encounter and MedicationRequest. Profiles constrain and extend how a resource is used in a particular implementation context. Use the relevant national or programme implementation guide rather than assuming that two systems use every resource in the same way.

Plan terminology and identifiers

Interoperability depends on consistent codes, units, value sets and identifiers. Define how local codes map to standard terminologies and how patient or organisation identifiers are reconciled. Avoid treating a display label as a stable code. Keep mappings versioned and review their impact when upstream systems change.

Protect access and consent

Authenticate systems and users, enforce least privilege and apply the applicable consent and purpose rules. Protect sensitive data in transit and at rest, minimise fields shared and record access. The exact legal basis and permitted disclosure vary by jurisdiction and organisational role; involve privacy and compliance specialists.

Build resilient integration behaviour

Handle retries, duplicate events, delayed results, partial failure and reconciliation. Use correlation identifiers and maintain traceability to the source record. A successful HTTP response does not prove that the receiving clinical workflow has accepted or reviewed the information.

Validate with domain experts

Use synthetic or appropriately de-identified data in development and testing. Validate resource structure, terminology, workflow semantics and safety implications with clinical informatics and operational teams. Software teams should not infer clinical meaning or make treatment decisions from the integration contract alone.

Key takeaway: FHIR provides a shared technical foundation, but safe interoperability still depends on local profiles, terminology, identity, consent and clinical workflow validation.
