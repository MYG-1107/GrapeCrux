EU AI Act Article 50: a 2026 transparency readiness checklist

Transparency is becoming an operational requirement for products that generate or manipulate text, images, audio and video. This guide explains how product and engineering teams can identify relevant AI interactions, design clear disclosures and preserve evidence without treating a checklist as legal advice.

Why Article 50 needs a product-level review

The EU AI Act includes transparency obligations for certain AI interactions and synthetic or manipulated content. These requirements affect user journeys, interface language, content pipelines, documentation and responsibilities—not just legal policy. The exact duty depends on the system, the organisation’s role, the use case and the applicable provision, so teams should map their actual product rather than assume every AI feature is treated identically.

The European Commission’s implementation timeline is progressive. As of 20 November 2026, the official timeline identifies a 2 December 2026 transition date for certain providers of AI systems generating synthetic content that were already placed on the market before 2 August 2026. Confirm whether that transition applies to your system with qualified counsel.

Inventory AI-generated experiences

List every user-facing feature that generates, transforms, summarizes or materially alters content: chat responses, image generation, audio synthesis, video editing, automated customer messages, voice interfaces and third-party AI components. Record the intended user, output type, geographic availability, model or vendor, launch date, provider/deployer relationship and accountable team.

Identify outputs that could be mistaken for authentic human or real-world material. Include downstream workflows: a generated image may be edited, downloaded, reposted or embedded in another product. Document where labels or provenance signals can be lost.

Design disclosures at the moment they matter

A transparency notice should be understandable in context. A short explanation near an AI-generated answer is often more useful than a broad statement buried in terms and conditions. For generated media, consider visible labels, embedded metadata or provenance signals where appropriate and technically feasible.

Do not rely on one badge for every use case. Test whether people understand what the system generated, what a person reviewed and what limitations remain. Make disclosures accessible to screen readers, keyboard users and mobile visitors.

Preserve evidence and use a release checklist

Keep versioned records of disclosure text, content pathways, model or service versions, approval rules and release dates. Minimize retained prompt and response content; define redaction, access and deletion rules. Support teams should know how to explain a disputed output and escalate suspected disclosure failures.

  • Inventory AI features, output types, users and regions.
  • Map provider and deployer responsibilities with legal review.
  • Identify applicable transparency and disclosure requirements.
  • Test plain-language notices and provenance handling.
  • Assign owners, preserve implementation evidence and review changes before release.

Make this a living product control, not a one-time document. Reassess when models, vendors, outputs or user journeys change.

Example: ship a disclosure as part of the release

Imagine a customer-support platform adding an AI feature that rewrites a customer's message into a shorter summary. The product team should document what content is transformed, whether the original is retained, who sees the summary and whether a human can correct it. The release checklist can include a disclosure requirement, accessibility review, sample outputs, logging rules and a test confirming that the notice remains visible on mobile and in exported content.

For a media-generation tool, the implementation may be different. Teams may need visible labels, metadata, or a provenance mechanism that survives file export and downstream editing. Test the actual user journey: a label that appears in the editor but disappears from a downloaded file may not meet the product's transparency goal.

Questions to settle before launch

  • Which outputs are generated or materially altered by an AI system?
  • Who is responsible for the system and for communicating with users?
  • Where will users see the disclosure, and can it be understood without technical knowledge?
  • What evidence demonstrates that the control is active in the released version?
  • Who reviews the design when the model, output format or distribution channel changes?

Keep the answers linked to product requirements and test cases so the control remains visible after the initial launch.
